what changes made me more productive?

November 5, 2026

also “I tried 43 crazy productivity hacks! You’ll be shocked by #12!!!”

Over the last 17 months, I’ve tried many different things to improve my productivity. What worked and what didn’t? I work in technical AI safety. Most of this time, my work looked primarily like running many empirical experiments, though it has recently been much more about writing and communicating with others.

This post is quickly written and not super considered.

I’ve organized this as a list with ratings and descriptions. The ratings are given according to the following scale:

  • 7 – Great on all accounts. Made me way more productive
  • 6 – Quite good
  • 5 – Weakly recommend, but it’s a minor improvement or somewhat costly to implement
  • 4 – Maybe reasonable, somewhat unclear
  • 3 – Doesn’t really seem to work
  • 2 – Likely reduced my productivity
  • 1 – Very bad for my productivity

Of course, I didn’t get to test these in any kind of rigorous way and many of them were tried with substantially lower sample size than might be optimal. These are my general impressions.

The list is organized as: Really Good, Actively Bad, Okay or Good, Unclear/No Change

The best of the best (6–7)

Blocking every website that could distract me — 7

Blocking things just works. I use Freedom↗.

One issue is that I frequently find other things to waste time on. I spent much more time on Substack after I blocked all the other things I was willing to read, and then after I blocked Substack, I would look at Curius↗ or read Clarkesworld magazine↗. This is annoying and probably points to some deeper desire to avoid work, but addressing the immediate issue still helps, because reading science fiction short stories is less addictive and less dopamine-filling than reading Twitter or Substack. Moreover, I feel happier afterwards than if I was on social media.

Staring at a wall — 7

This is just so unbelievably helpful for everything in my life. I still don’t do it that often because it’s not fun, but this is stupid because it’s so good. Usually, I try to stare at the wall for around 30 minutes.

One way this strategy can work well is if I have a set of questions I need to answer. Things like:

  • Are there ways that I can be taking on more risk?
  • What should various people around me be working on?

However, it’s still very valuable (maybe more valuable) without the questions as long as I commit to thinking about things that matter to me. Multiple important changes in my life are downstream of a random thought entering my mind while I stared at the wall. These can really be anything, but two less-significant examples include realizing that I should spend significantly more time on a hobby I enjoy or realizing a much more interesting interpretation of a short story I like. My main guesses for why this is useful are:

  • I spend very little time on unstructured thinking without distractions, so even a small amount is high-value
  • I get out-of-distribution thoughts: there are many things that are valuable to consider but aren’t related to a particular experience, such that they can only come up unprompted
  • I can also do interdisciplinary connections. By default, I don’t connect unrelated areas of my life if I’m focused on only one of them
  • It makes me generally more patient and less distractable

I’m not allowed to have a book. I’m not allowed to look out a window because then I’ll stare at the birds, which are cute, and stare at the cars, which are moving very fast. I don’t listen to music because I’ll think about the music and even if I don’t the music will take up some amount of the background cognition happening in my mind. I try to put myself in the least stimulating circumstances I possibly can. It’s okay if I have a notepad to write things down but I shouldn’t use it to do other things like draw. As the title says, I aim to just be staring at the wall.

Becoming someone who habitually works all the time — 7

A big portion of my productivity comes from how I view work and my relationship to it. I’ve been the most productive when working felt like my natural state. I would default to working whenever I could, not because I was stressed or overworked, but because that seemed like a reasonable thing to do. Viewing work as something that required willpower makes it require more willpower.

It’s not 100% clear how someone can build and maintain a habit like this—I’ve never maintained this state for longer than 4-5 months, usually because I would find something really exciting to do outside of work, which collapses the mental state. Also, the downsides are quite costly—I don’t think this kind of mindset can easily coexist with hobbies or romantic relationships, though some people have clearly made it work. Also, it might make it harder to compartmentalize when work is stressful.

Using 5 minute timers — 6

See Neel Nanda here↗.

Set a timer and spend that time trying to write down anything that comes to mind. This doesn’t have a straightforward application to everything, especially long tasks which resist being decomposed, but that set is surprisingly small.

I do this whenever I need to:

  • Produce a list of ideas
  • Break down a task to its component steps
  • Outline a blogpost or paper
  • Work on something which I’ve been struggling to start

Working at night — 6

When trying to produce individual-contributor-style work, I am much more productive when working at times that are extremely de-correlated from everyone else. [I’ve had to give this up for meetings at work]

Between 10:00 pm and 3:00 am, nobody else is in the office: the CO2 levels are low, there are very few distractions, I can’t talk to anyone, and there are no meetings to break up the flow.

Probably much of this is specific to me: I strongly prefer the nighttime and cold temperatures, and can maintain unorthodox sleep schedules indefinitely.

There are many downsides here unrelated to productivity:

  • I’m not able to meet with people in the morning
  • I have nights where I sleep poorly because an event is scheduled in the early morning
  • I get less sunlight
  • I need to use blackout curtains or an eye mask
  • Whenever they visit, my parents harass me for being a lazy bum who will clearly be fired for coming in so late

Eating less for lunch — 6

Eating a big lunch is non-ideal because I get really tired afterwards. I don’t feel any real downsides from eating a smaller lunch except needing a slightly larger dinner. Alternatively, I’ve heard good things from people about buying a glucose monitor and/or trying to avoid carbohydrates, though I haven’t attempted this in any real way.

Optimizing my keyboard shortcuts — 6

There are certain keyboard shortcuts which make my life considerably easier. People get less value from this than they should because they look for marginal improvements to their current workflow. Instead, I’ve gotten the most value from thinking about workflows I (unknown to me) wasn’t adopting because some action was high friction and then removing that friction. This is harder, but it provides larger gains. Watching other people work (below) is helpful for ideation.

For example, I reference files far more than most people when I code because I have efficient shortcuts for referencing directories. I also often see coworkers browsing through masses of tabs rather than having search-focused organization or browser go links for frequently referenced pages.

Starting relatively short sprints and telling my boss to enforce them — 6

I have tried this three times and all of them highly improved my productivity.

In most cases, I am best positioned to decide what is reasonable to accomplish in two weeks and my boss has less insight than me, such that he is in a worse position to set or enforce deadlines and doesn’t. In each case, I thought of the highest priority things to do, how quickly I could do them if I made them my top priority and cleared away other distractions, and pitched my manager on having a single-focus sprint.

Watching a friend work for 30 minutes and taking notes — 6

This isn’t really a productivity hack, but it did make me better at my job. I picked up on a number of patterns or workflows they follow that simplified my work significantly.

Other friends have tried the opposite, where a coworker watches you and gives tips, but I think it works less well because your coworker has less context on how you work and their advice will generally be marginal improvements or costly (in attention) changes. Watching someone else, you can contrast their full set of workflows with your own and consider what you should change yourself.

Doing pomodoros — 5.5–6

I’ve often worked in pomodoro blocks for the last 16 month period, though many days drifted out of the enforced structure into a more free-form structure. The classic pomodoro length is 25 working - 5 resting, but I found this too short and impossible to work within. Instead, I usually did blocks of 45-10. In certain cases, shrinking the period of enforced work was helpful for getting stuff done while very distracted, tired, or unmotivated.

Having breaks frequently revealed that I was deep in an unimportant rabbit hole or should prioritize differently. Unfortunately, by adding a defined stopping time, pomodoros also stopped me when I was locked-in and made it easier to stop and wander off.

Unlike most productivity interventions there’s some empirical research here that people can go look at. I haven’t.

Actively unhelpful (1–2)

Drinking caffeine — 2.5

My experience with caffeine has been that it tastes really good. The default effect is I get a marginal improvement in productivity in the near term with a larger decrease in productivity through the day.

Also, caffeinated drinks are usually quite acidic and acidity causes me a bunch of negative effects. I still drink them because I really like how they taste but this is unendorsed behavior 😱. I have also tried caffeine pills, but they give me bad headaches.

Working out in the morning — 2

Other people really like this, but I feel really tired after working out in the morning and don’t recover until much later in the day. I’m also just not really a morning person.

Playing subway surfers videos on my second monitor — 2

Yeah I mean, did you think this would make me more productive?

Just trying harder — 2

Obligatory link to a great Neel Nanda blogpost.↗

This is the most insidious, common pitfall I fall into. Every time I resolve to work even harder this is a failure. My best productivity has always come from when working didn’t feel that strenuous and maybe even a little fun. Once I started viewing myself as needing more willpower to work, I needed to use that willpower to work, but didn’t see any actual productivity gains.

A useful corollary, when I feel like working is very aversive, that’s a strong signal to do something else or to find some other way to make it less unpleasant (e.g. coworking with some friends).

Working in a crowded office kitchen area filled with people — 1

Yeah this is just obviously a bad idea that I still do once or twice every month in some sort of crazy delusional state.

  • I get distracted by tons of people.
  • I’m not focused on my work.
  • The people walking by are also not working during the workday so they’re adversely selected to be fun, sociable, and generators of unproductivity.
  • I know I will be distracted, so I don’t even attempt any real thinking or longer tasks.
  • I constantly want to get up to eat.
  • The area around me is loud.
  • I’m sitting in an uncomfortable chair.
  • I don’t have a monitor.

Things That Were Okay (5)

Working with a monitor and keyboard — 5.5

This is very helpful for:

  • Dev work
  • Running experiments
  • Switching between many different things

But pretty negligible on tasks like:

  • Deep thinking about some question
  • Writing docs
  • Planning out future work

My work splits kind of evenly between these categories. [No longer true, such that having a monitor is less useful]

Semi-related aside: I cannot understand using a laptop screen as a second monitor. Just get another monitor. The laptop screen is worse than a different non-laptop monitor. There is an unlimited supply of monitors [maybe this is false, I haven’t checked].

Taking days off — 5.5

Excluding the low-willpower work-maxxing state I describe above, I find working six days a week much more sustainable than seven days a week for any amount of time. Many of my friends seem to see the same step change at five days a week. I worry many of my friends incorrectly model the long-term costs of never taking any days off. Personally, if I do any amount of work on a day, it’s a ~workday and will be somewhat mentally taxing for me. Even if I don’t work, but I was planning to and I slack off, I find the experience somewhat draining. The only way for me to really feel rested is to take the entire day off and know the whole time that I’m not going to work.

Even though this is incompatible with “Becoming someone who habitually works all the time” above, my guess is that it is easier to target and maintain and should probably be the default for most people.

Not listening to music while working — 5.5

Not listening to music is probably strictly better than listening to music while I work. Listening to music makes it much harder for me to think.

I still frequently listen to music while I work though because I like listening to music so much and I’m incapable of taking obvious good advice. So far, I think classical and soundtrack music is less distracting than pop/rock/hiphop etc. and new music is much more distracting than music I’ve heard many times before, but all are somewhat bad.

Doing work with money on the line — 5.5

Usually I do this by making a bet with a friend that I will accomplish X task by Y time or pay them Z dollars.

I need to choose an amount of money that actually motivates me. Unfortunately, after a while I’ve gotten a little bit habituated to losing money in this fashion, but it still makes me more productive.

Some of my friends have tried this to success, but a few have had this pitfall where they really want to succeed so they bet a really large amount of money, like a thousand dollars. This has never worked because they’re not actually willing to pay that amount of money and their counterparty (some friend of theirs) will feel uncomfortable and won’t make them pay out on a bet that large. I personally usually use $100, which is pretty large but also an amount my friends are willing to take from me.

Mainly, this acts on me like a pop-up that there are things on the line hacking my brain into remembering that I need to go to my computer and work.

Cutting out kinda-work — 5.5

This is a 7 for many people, but I’ve never had too much of an issue with it. It’s really important to prioritize work well and really bad to waste time on tasks that feel easy and are not that important. It’s tiring and drains mental energy, but doesn’t actually do what matters.

Working in two larger timeblocks with non-work stuff in the middle — 5

This is a pretty marginal improvement but it does work well when running a lot of empirical experiments. It’s nice to start a bunch of runs, let them run for a while and then come back, look at how they’re going, and process some of the results. Often I’ll spot errors that I missed before.

This works well if I am also working at night, because I can:

  • show up late,
  • have a bunch of meetings around 11:00AM,
  • work until like 6:00-7:00PM or something
  • go spend some time with my friends at the point of the day I feel most tired
  • come back around 9:00-10:00PM, and work until like 2:00AM.

This might not make me that much more productive but it is a marginally better layout than working in one long block. Taking a long break felt nice and refreshing.

Taking vacation — 5

This depends heavily on what I’m doing: many vacations aren’t that restful to me. Frequently, vacations look like going on trips and talking to people a lot, which is probably energizing for some people but is pretty tiring for me.

I’d like it to be new-ish, to remove me from my comfort zone, and to not be stressful or exhausting. One thing that worked for me recently:

  • Flying to my friend’s house across the US and doing nothing but playing board games with them for a week

Taking frequent walks — 5

Overrated alert: I expected this to be really good and I still do it a lot because it’s just really pleasant, but I think taking a walk mostly just makes me take a walk. I’m not that much more productive after I’ve taken the walk. Other people report this being really good for improving their mental state. Maybe there are other walking methods which are better for being recharged.

Still in 5 because I like walking :)

Coworking with my friends — 5

This changes based on the friend. Coworking with hard-working grinders did make it easier for me to get things done. Coworking with my high-school and college friends is mostly just a very obvious excuse to hang out and I’m not going to get anything done unless I put a lot of effort into ignoring them.

Telling lots of people that I’m really busy and need to work — 5

This doesn’t really do anything but it does create some kind of nice incentives.

When I wander about and talk to friends and I’ve previously spent a bunch of time complaining about how I really need to work, they make fun of me and my pretend dilemma. I start to think, “Huh, it’s embarrassing if I go out of my office and twirl in circles and eat snacks and chat when I’ve already claimed to have a lot of work to do, so I should go do that work.”

Tracking what I do every 30 minutes — 5

I’ve done this for a combined 1.5 years, partially recently and partially during college.

It creates a nice colorful chart that I know I will show friends where work-y things are colored green and wasting time is colored red. This makes a nice incentive to maximize green squares (and blue/purple squares representing things like sleep, spending time with friends, and other gainful experiences) and minimize embarrassing red ones.

Spending all my willpower/cognition on extensively scoping out my work — 5

This works well. If a task is decomposable to simple steps and I have written out my task well, the remaining work becomes much, much easier. The main issue is that some work is hard to break apart (e.g. figuring out the answer to some difficult conceptual question). However, attempting it still seems valuable.

Working while doing other stuff — 4.5–5

Some of the most productive periods I’ve ever had I spent very little actual time working. For example, on Labor Day in 2025, I went on a road trip with a friend and also had a number of deadlines the next week. I of course committed to not spending any actual trip time where my friend was awake and available working because that would have been a boring loser thing to do. However, anytime he was occupied with something else I’d put my computer out and frantically try to get as many experiments running as possible. Examples of times I was working include:

  • While my friend was asleep in the hotel room
  • While my friend was getting the car from the rental agency
  • On my phone while I was at the restaurant before my friend had arrived at the restaurant

I was really really productive given how little I was working and I got a surprising number of experiments done in those few days. Unfortunately, it did require me to spend some amount of time thinking about things and pre-organizing my work such that it would be really easy for me to launch and oversee experiments during the trip. I also was not able to do any kind of substantial work like thinking or writing, only check on experiments and run new experiments. If I was in a more exploratory phase or conceptual part of the project, this wouldn’t have worked. Also while I got lucky and didn’t make any mistakes, probably this strategy generally leads to a bunch of issues.

Things That Didn’t Work (3–4)

Conditioning myself to work harder with specific music — 4.5

This kind of works. I don’t really do this that much though. I have a friend who has listened to “Fred again.. Boiler Room” 10,000 times over the past year, every single time they work.

It seems really bad to listen to new music while working but if I listen to music that I am extremely familiar with, it affects my brain a lot less. My guess is that, unfortunately, this probably makes me literally less efficient at working. However, it does seem like an okay trick as an ‘on-switch’ that it’s time to work, justifying the medium rating.

Waking up early to work in the morning — 4.5

This is the one that everyone likes, but it’s just so much worse than working at night. People like it because there’s some kind of common shared belief that being up in the morning is virtuous.

Morning work is worse than night work because there are many more situations where I am incentivized to stay up late (like hanging out with my friends) and thus ruin my sleep than there are situations where I need to wake up early.

There are plenty of times where I go to a party or out with people or I need to grind out some work for a deadline. This forces me to stay up quite late. However, there’s nothing that really forces me to wake up early in the morning except for flights and occasionally people wanting to go for hikes or play Twilight Imperium or something.

Pivoting fast when I’m feeling unproductive — 4

One theory of unproductive activity is that when I’m feeling unproductive, I should not attempt to work, but should instead go do something else. This way I work when motivated and productive and have extremely efficient work hours. When I’m not feeling productive, I have rest hours rather than trying to work and ending up working inefficiently.

This seems on the extremes correct. If I’m sick, I should not work. If I am unbelievably tired or unbelievably burnt out, I should take time off. However, on a daily basis, this feels like it lets my brain hack to “feeling unproductive” and then I don’t get work done.

Getting into the habit of working lets me work even when I feel unproductive. I don’t want to have my productivity vary more than it already does in the thrall of my changing emotions or moods.

Drawing many Pokémon on the whiteboard — 4

Yeah it didn’t make me more productive but it was very fun.

Praying to God — 4

I mean, this usually doesn’t hurt.

Setting an extremely rigid schedule — 3

A lot of people try this and my impression is that it just doesn’t work for me. I don’t have an extremely low variance workplace, so things frequently shift.

It seems good to have rigid times for certain things, like when I sleep and when I wake up. Presumably also meals, but actually planning out when I do my work is hard and often futile.

Shortening the workday — 3

“Oh I’m not getting that much productivity out of my time. I’m in the office a lot but I feel like I’m distracted and not working that much. Maybe the solution is that if I have less time to work and I restrict myself to only being in the office from hour A to hour B, I will be forced to work more efficiently.”

This maybe has a slight positive effect on work efficiency but mostly just reduces the amount of time that I’m working.

I am a fan of taking weekends off, taking vacation, leaving after lunch on Wednesday or something. Moderately shortening hours doesn’t look like a big shift, so it mostly just causes me to work less.

Basically none of my friends who have done this actually found more productivity or significantly more efficient productivity. The intuition that sometimes people should work less is correct but this is a bad way of doing it.

Working out generally — 3

Over long stretches of multiple months I haven’t noticed any real increase in productivity when I’m working out versus not working out consistently. I think that there are a bunch of other great reasons to work out but these don’t hinge on my productivity.

Frequently changing my desk — 3

I’ve had periods where I felt, “Oh there’s something about my desk which makes me feel unproductive. I don’t like being at my desk,” and then I would go try working at a different desk.

Changing my environment wasn’t a terrible idea but it became obvious pretty quickly that I was actually just avoiding work. I was not more productive at the other desks than I was at my own desk. Maybe I weakly endorse changing desk every six months or something to avoid building up a strong distaste for my desk but if that’s not an issue then I don’t worry about it.

Having a rival to compete with — 3

I don’t really care about this at all. Maybe other people find it really motivating?

Feeling more scared about AI — 3

Surprisingly enough this hasn’t really changed that much. Being stressed or under pressure makes me feel bad, but there’s no rise in performance. It is very important to care deeply about my work, which I do and have.

Being intellectually excited about my work — 3

Same as above. This is very different for many of my friends, but honestly if something is instrumentally useful, I will be excited to do it.

Changing my phone background and computer background to motivational/urgency images — 3

I immediately filter them out and stop realizing I did that.

Promising myself rewards if I achieve some goal — 3

This is the worse version of making a bet. I irrationally care much more about losing money than about gaining it, so I should use that asymmetry to motivate myself.

So what did I learn and how does this transfer?

I understand myself and my own work-habits significantly better than I did. I’ve become much better at avoiding counterproductive habits and at working in a more sustainable, steady pattern.

Unfortunately, my guess is that much of this doesn’t transfer, especially since I structured it as ‘what worked for me’ rather than a recommendation to others. Many tips from my friends and mentors didn’t end up working for me. However, there were a couple main ideas that took me time to internalize and are probably general.

  • Changes that make work need less willpower are the most useful and trying to exert more willpower usually fails
  • Distractions do reduce productivity
  • Structure your day so that you have blocks of time to work when you feel most energetic and are unlikely to be interrupted

Beyond that, my best advice is to try things and learn! :)
